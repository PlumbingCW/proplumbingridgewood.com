How a failing service line shows itself.

A service line fails quietly for a long time and then all at once. Because it is buried, most people notice the effects before the pipe.

These are the signs we hear about most, usually in this order.

  • Water pressure that has dropped at every fixture, not just one faucet.
  • A soft, soggy or unusually green strip of lawn between the street and the house.
  • A water meter that keeps moving after every tap and appliance is off.
  • A hissing or rushing sound where the line enters the basement wall.
  • Muddy or discolored water right after pressure dips.
  • A water bill that has climbed with no change in how you live.

What to do before we arrive when the line is leaking.

A service line leak can flood a basement fast. These steps slow it down.

Shut the main valve.

Find the main shutoff where the line enters the house, usually in the basement near the meter, and turn it fully closed. If the handle is stuck, do not force it with a wrench.

Check the meter.

With everything off, watch the meter dial. If it still moves after the main valve is closed on the street side, the leak is outside before your valve.

Clear the entry area.

Move boxes, stored items and electronics away from the wall where the pipe comes in, and unplug anything near standing water.

Keep the shutoff key near.

If the meter pit has a curb stop, do not open it yourself. Note where it is and tell us on the call.

Who owns which part of the line.

In Ridgewood the pipe is split in two. The public side, from the main to the curb, belongs to Ridgewood Water, the Village utility. The private side, from the curb to your building, belongs to you.

That split matters on the first call. A leak on the public side is a utility repair. A leak on the private side is yours to fix, and a licensed plumber can usually tell which side by looking at the meter, the shutoff and the wet ground.

If you are unsure, call Ridgewood Water at 201-670-5520 to report the leak while we plan the private-side work.

Lead service lines and New Jersey's 2031 deadline.

New Jersey law requires water systems to find and remove all lead and galvanized service lines by 2031. Ridgewood Water has identified more than 3,000 lines that are wholly or partly lead, and the utility runs a replacement program with an opt-in and opt-out step for customers who are notified.

If you hire your own contractor for the private side, the work has to be coordinated with Ridgewood Water at 201-670-5520. That keeps the public and private halves matched so you do not end up with a new pipe joined to an old lead one.

Before any digging, we confirm the pipe material at the entry point. A magnet and a scratch test tell a lot, and the utility's notice tells the rest.

Depth matters in northern New Jersey. According to the frost depth table in the state construction code, the northern half of New Jersey uses three feet, so any new line has to be buried below the freeze zone. A shallow old pipe is one of the reasons a service line splits in a hard winter, a failure that overlaps with burst pipe repair.

When the line is damaged, it is also worth checking the inside piping, because pressure surges after repair can show weak spots. A leak detection visit before the trench closes can save a second dig. For a home in Midland Park, which is also served by Ridgewood Water, see the Midland Park page.
