Clues that point to a hidden leak.

A bill that keeps creeping up.

If usage rises with no change in your household, water is going somewhere. Check a few months of bills against each other.

A meter that never stops.

With every tap and appliance off, a moving meter dial means water is leaving the system.

Damp, stained or bubbling surfaces.

Brown rings on a ceiling, paint that peels, warped baseboards or a musty smell often trace back to a slow pipe leak.

Water noise with nothing running.

Hissing or trickling in a wall suggests a pressurized line is leaking. Low pressure at fixtures can say the same thing.

Any one of these is worth testing. Two together mean it is time to call, because slow leaks rot framing and feed mold while you wait.

A meter test you can run tonight.

  1. Turn off every tap, the dishwasher, the washer and the ice maker. Make sure nobody flushes..
  2. Find your water meter and note the reading, or watch the small leak indicator dial if it has one..
  3. Wait fifteen minutes without using any water, then look again..
  4. If the reading moved, water is escaping. Close the main shutoff and check whether the meter stops. If it does, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the line between the meter and the shutoff..

That last result is the most useful clue you can give a plumber. It cuts the search in half before we arrive.

Emergency steps if water is escaping right now.

An active leak should be stopped and contained first, then located.

Close the main shutoff.

Find the valve where the water line enters the house, often near the meter in a basement. Turn it clockwise until it stops.

Open the lowest tap.

With the main off, run a faucet to relieve pressure and drain what remains in the pipes.

Cut power near water.

If water is near outlets, an appliance or the electrical panel, turn off the breaker for that area, but only if you can reach it dry.

Photograph and mop up.

Take photos of stains and wet areas for your records, then remove standing water and open windows to help drying.

Where the water goes in Ridgewood.

According to the New Jersey Infrastructure Bank, Ridgewood Water has identified more than 3,000 service lines that are wholly or partly lead, and state law requires lead and galvanized service lines to be found and removed by 2031. The utility owns the section from the main to the curb, and the homeowner owns the stretch from the curb to the building.

That private stretch is where a meter leak often hides, because it is buried and out of sight. If we find an older line failing there, the fix may be tied to the lead line replacement process, and we coordinate with Ridgewood Water as the program requires. For that work, see water service line repair.

Frost depth matters too. The state construction code table sets 36 inches of frost depth for northern New Jersey, and water lines are meant to be buried below that depth. A line laid shallow or disturbed by later digging is more exposed in a hard freeze. A frozen split near the surface is a different story, and burst pipe repair covers it.
