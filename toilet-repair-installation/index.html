Toilet problems we are called for most.

Overflowing bowl.

Water rising to the rim and spilling means the drain is blocked below the trap or in the line. Stop flushing first, then call.

Leak at the base.

Water around the floor flange, a rocking toilet or a stained ceiling below usually means a failed wax ring or cracked flange.

Running tank.

A flapper that does not seal, a bad fill valve or a worn chain lets water trickle into the bowl all day.

Weak flush or gurgling.

Partial clogs, blocked rim jets or a venting issue give a lazy flush. Gurgling in other fixtures points toward the main line.

Cracked tank or bowl.

A hairline crack in porcelain can leak slowly and give way later. These are replaced rather than patched.

If the toilet is overflowing, do this before we get there.

You can stop most overflows in under a minute. Do it in this order.

Lift the tank lid.

Push the flapper down into the seat, or lift the float up. That stops water from refilling the bowl.

Close the shutoff valve.

Turn the oval or round handle on the wall or floor behind the toilet clockwise. If it will not turn, shut the main water valve instead.

Do not plunge a full bowl twice.

One careful plunge with a flange plunger is fine. Repeated pumping on a blocked drain can push waste into the tub.

Protect the floor.

Lay towels at the base, lift rugs and keep the bathroom door closed for children. Check the ceiling below for stains if the leak ran on a second floor.

Finding the real cause, not just the symptom.

A toilet that overflows once may only have a wad of paper. A toilet that overflows twice in a month has a bigger problem. We check whether one toilet is blocked or several fixtures are, because that tells us if the fix is a closet auger or a visit for sewer line cleaning.

For a leak, we lift the toilet and look at the flange, the bolts and the floor. Soft wood under a leaking base is a repair of its own, and it is better found now than after the subfloor rots.

Replacing a toilet instead of repairing it.

Some toilets are not worth another repair. A cracked tank, a chronic clog from an old design or a base that keeps rocking on a damaged flange are good reasons to replace.

A new toilet changes more than the look. Matching the rough-in distance, the seat height and the bowl shape to the person using it matters. We bring the measurements we need and confirm the flange before the old unit comes out.

Usually repair.

  • Seat or bolt issues.

Usually replace.

  • Cracked tank or bowl.
  • Repeated clogs from a poor design.
  • Damaged flange that cannot hold the seal.
  • A fixture older than the flooring around it.

Why Ridgewood homes see more toilet wear.

Census data puts the average Ridgewood household at 2.93 people, and about 82.5 percent of homes are owner-occupied. Larger households flush more often, and owners usually plan to stay and want the fix to last.

That adds up to hardworking toilets, with flappers and fill valves doing thousands of cycles a year. A cheap rubber flapper that warps after a season will cost more in wasted water than a quality part costs to install.

If a toilet you replace sits next to another bathroom fixture that is also dated, the same visit can often cover a faucet repair. For homes in Glen Rock, the Glen Rock page has nearby coverage.
