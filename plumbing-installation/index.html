What counts as plumbing installation.

Installation is new work, as opposed to repairing something that broke. It covers adding a fixture, moving one, hooking up an appliance and building the supply and drain lines for a space that did not have them.

If something is already failing, our leak detection and repair pages fit better. If you are replacing a heater, see water heater installation. This page is for the rest of the planned work.

Planning the rough-in before the walls close.

Rough-in is the stage where pipes are placed inside open framing. Mistakes here are expensive, because fixing them means opening finished surfaces. Decide fixture locations, vanity sizes and shower valve heights before the plumber arrives.

Bring your contractor, designer or tile setter into the conversation early. Venting, slope for drains and framing clearances all influence where fixtures can go. A drain that needs a quarter inch per foot of fall can limit a basement bathroom more than any design choice.

Code, licensing and inspections in New Jersey.

New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code adopts the 2021 National Standard Plumbing Code as its plumbing subcode. That is the standard your work is inspected against. Ridgewood's Department of Building and Inspections enforces it, with a Construction Official and a Plumbing Subcode Official.

State law allows plumbing to be done only by a master plumber or an authorized plumbing contractor, or by employees of one. You can check a license on the Division of Consumer Affairs' online verification portal. Licenses renew every two years, so ask for a current one.

The code treats some work as minor. Adding new fixtures in existing dwelling space, without increasing the size of the water service or the house drain, counts. The agency must still be notified, a permit application filed within five business days and the work inspected. Bigger changes need approval before work starts, and we sort out the category up front.

Older Ridgewood neighborhoods and what a remodel can uncover.

The Ridgewood Public Library's local-history pages date Brookside to about 1890, The Heights to between 1892 and 1930, and The Lawns to 1950, when 429 Cape Cods were built on Grove Street. Homes from those eras have seen decades of changes behind the walls.

That has a practical meaning for an installation. When we open a wall for a new bathroom, we may find supply lines, drains or vents of different ages and materials. We assess what is there before tying new work to it, and we tell you if a section should be replaced while the wall is open.

Doing it new versus adapting what is there.

Adapting existing lines works when.

  • Fixtures are moving only a short distance.
  • Old piping is in good condition.
  • The drain slope and vent are adequate.
  • The job is a one-for-one swap.

New lines make sense when.

  • You are adding a bathroom, kitchen or laundry.
  • The existing pipe is galvanized or leaking.
  • Fixtures are moving to another wall or floor.
  • A layout change needs new venting.

When older supply pipes are the problem, a bigger repiping project may save you from opening the same wall twice. If water reaches a basement, see basement waterproofing before you finish that space.
